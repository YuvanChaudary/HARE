# Heat Risk Factors — Heat Action Response Engine MVP

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the multidimensional factors that contribute to occupational heat risk within the **Heat Action Response Engine**.

A common failure in workplace safety management is reducing heat risk to a single environmental measurement (such as dry-bulb air temperature). In reality, physiological heat strain is the cumulative product of atmospheric conditions, metabolic heat generated by physical labor, radiative solar loads, clothing barriers, hydration availability, and organizational response readiness.

## 2. Environmental Factors

Environmental factors describe ambient atmospheric conditions surrounding the worksite:

### 2.1 Dry-Bulb Air Temperature ($T_a$)
* **Definition:** Ambient temperature measured by a shielded thermometer in the open air.
* **Operational Significance:** When ambient air temperature exceeds human skin temperature ($\approx 35^\circ\text{C}$), convective heat loss ceases, and the human body absorbs convective heat from the surrounding environment.

### 2.2 Relative Humidity ($RH$)
* **Definition:** The ratio of water vapor present in the air relative to the saturation point at that temperature.
* **Operational Significance:** Evaporation of sweat is the human body's primary physiological mechanism for dissipating heat. High relative humidity sharply impairs evaporative cooling efficiency.

### 2.3 Radiant Heat & Solar Exposure
* **Definition:** Thermal radiation emitted by the sun or reradiated by hot physical surfaces (e.g., asphalt, steel plating, structural concrete).
* **Operational Significance:** Direct sunlight can increase the effective thermal load on a worker by several degrees Celsius compared to shaded ambient air.

### 2.4 Air Movement / Wind Speed ($v$)
* **Definition:** The velocity of airflow across the human body.
* **Operational Significance:** Moderate air movement enhances evaporative sweat cooling up to a physiological threshold; however, in extremely hot air ($T_a > 38^\circ\text{C}$), high wind speeds increase convective heat transfer into the body.

### 2.5 Diurnal Trends & Duration of Elevated Conditions
* **Definition:** The cumulative duration of sustained elevated temperatures during an operational shift.
* **Operational Significance:** Sustained exposure without adequate rest leads to cumulative cardiovascular strain and progressive dehydration.

## 3. Workload & Metabolic Factors

Human physical labor generates significant internal metabolic heat that must be continuously dissipated to maintain thermal equilibrium:

* **`LIGHT` Workload ($\approx 100\text{--}200\text{ W}$):** Machine operation, inspection, seated assembly, light handheld power tools. Low internal heat generation.
* **`MODERATE` Workload ($\approx 200\text{--}350\text{ W}$):** Sustained walking carrying light materials, equipment maintenance, plumbing, drywall installation. Moderate internal heat generation.
* **`HEAVY` Workload ($\approx 350\text{--}500\text{ W}$):** Manual shoveling, wheelbarrow transport, manual concrete placement, structural rebar tying, heavy timber framing. High internal heat generation.
* **`VERY_HEAVY` Workload ($> 500\text{ W}$):** Rapid intense physical exertion, continuous uphill carrying of heavy loads, trench digging in direct sun. Severe internal heat generation.
* **`UNKNOWN` Workload:** Unclassified operational state prompting administrative verification.

> **Operational Distinction:** Workload is an operational category describing job physical intensity. It is **not** an individual biometric measurement or worker heart-rate reading.

## 4. Exposure Factors

Exposure factors describe how workers interact with the physical job site environment over time:

* **Outdoor Direct Sun:** Unrestricted exposure to solar radiation with minimal overhead protection.
* **Outdoor Shaded / Scaffolded:** Work under protective netting, scaffolding decks, or temporary awnings that mitigate radiant heat.
* **Indoor Semi-Conditioned / Unconditioned:** Work in large metal warehouses, manufacturing bays, or boiler rooms where air circulation is poor and roofs radiate absorbed heat.
* **Continuous vs. Intermittent Exposure:** Long uninterrupted work bouts without scheduled shade pauses drastically accelerate thermal strain.
* **Shift Timing:** Work scheduled during peak midday solar radiation (e.g., 12:00–15:30) carries significantly higher risk than identical work performed during early morning hours.

## 5. Protective Clothing and PPE

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) is frequently mandatory for construction, industrial, and electrical trades, but heavily influences thermal strain:

* **Standard Breathable Cotton:** Allows natural air movement and relatively unobstructed sweat evaporation.
* **Heavy Flame-Retardant (FR) Coveralls:** Thicker fabric reduces convective cooling and slows evaporative dissipation.
* **Impermeable Chemical / Vapor-Barrier Suits:** Traps moisture and warm air entirely, preventing evaporative sweat cooling regardless of ambient humidity.
* **High-Visibility Heavy Vests & Hard Hats:** Add localized thermal insulation to the torso and head.

> **Policy on Numerical Clothing Adjustments:** The MVP does not apply arbitrary mathematical clothing adjustment factors unless they are derived directly from validated, authoritative occupational safety standards (e.g., published ISO or NIOSH guidelines). In the MVP, impermeable PPE acts as an operational trigger elevating the contextual risk tier.

## 6. Cooling and Hydration Mitigations

On-site controls directly reduce thermal strain and mitigate environmental heat risks:

* **Potable Water Proximity:** Water stations located within a 2-minute walking radius increase actual fluid intake compared to distant water sources.
* **Chilled Water & Electrolytes:** Chilled water encourages greater fluid consumption and provides minor internal conductive cooling.
* **Deployable Shaded Shelters:** Clean, uncrowded rest canopies allow radiant body cooling between physical labor rotations.
* **Active Cooling Assets:** Misting fans, evaporative coolers, or air-conditioned rest trailers enhance recovery kinetics during scheduled rest breaks.

## 7. Acclimatization Context

Physiological acclimatization represents the body's natural adaptation to working in hot conditions over 7 to 14 days of gradual exposure:

* **Established Workforce:** Crews that have worked continuously in the local seasonal climate for several weeks.
* **New or Returning Workforce:** Workers newly hired, mobilized from cooler regions, or returning from extended absences (illness, leave), who are significantly more susceptible to heat strain.

## 8. Organizational & Response Factors

Operational risk is ultimately determined by an organization's capacity to recognize hazards and act:

* **Supervisor Presence:** On-site supervision to monitor crew physical welfare.
* **Explicit Action Ownership:** Assigning preventive directives to identified individuals rather than general broadcasts.
* **Prompt Acknowledgment:** Verifying that field supervisors are aware of current conditions.
* **Structured Escalation:** Failsafe alerting when supervisors fail to respond, ensuring directives are not orphaned.
* **Clear Field Communication:** Converting technical heat indices into clear spoken briefings for crews.

## 10. Critical Multidimensional Principle

> **Heat risk is multidimensional. A single weather metric should not automatically be treated as a complete occupational heat-risk assessment.**

An ambient temperature of 34°C during a light assembly task in a well-ventilated, shaded shop represents a vastly different operational reality than 34°C during heavy concrete wheelbarrow transport in direct sunlight. The Heat Action Response Engine combines environmental signals with operational worksite context to produce an accurate, actionable occupational risk assessment.

---

## 11. Design Principle

> **Effective heat-risk operations require assessing the interaction between the worker, the task, the physical environment, and available organizational controls. Evaluating environmental data in isolation creates dangerous operational blind spots.**
